A panel for Home Assistant. An app for Apple Home or Home Assistant.

A picture of your home.
Until you reach for it.

Hauser turns a wall tablet or your phone into a calm view of your home. One room filling the screen, or all of them at once — either way the picture is the state: it follows the sun, the weather and the lamps that are on. Touch it, and the controls come forward. On the wall it runs on Home Assistant. In your pocket it is a native iOS app that also runs with Apple Home, no server needed.

Two views of the same home screen, both captured from the running app. After dark the all-rooms view reads from across the hallway: a room with a lamp on carries its lit picture, a room with every lamp out carries the dark one. Your own rooms are drawn from a photograph you take with your phone.

  • It sits on top of Home Assistant

    Hauser does not replace your setup, it gives it a face. Install it as an add-on on Home Assistant OS, or with Docker Compose anywhere else. A guided setup reads your areas and devices, proposes rooms and lets you correct the result. And it keeps following: move a device to another area in Home Assistant and it moves room in Hauser, without a reload. No YAML, no source edits.

  • Made for the wall and for the hand

    On a wall panel or tablet you pick the view: one room filling the screen with a narrow control surface beside it, or all rooms as tiles side by side. On a phone the rooms are the same cards under your thumb — in the browser, or in the iOS app, which also runs directly with Apple Home. Lights, heating, blinds, media, the calendar, the shopping list and the household's notes are all there, in six languages.

  • Yours, and nobody else's

    It runs on your own hardware and talks only to your Home Assistant or your Apple Home. No account, no cloud service, no telemetry. Code, design tokens and documentation are open source under the AGPL.

The house is the stage.
The interface is the light on it.

A wall panel is touched for perhaps five per cent of its day. Most home dashboards are designed for those five per cent: tiles, numbers, a grid. Hauser is designed for the other ninety-five. It is the first smart-home interface that people describe not as practical, but as calm and beautiful. Whether that holds is decided in three moments.

01 · The glance

Most of the day, the panel is a picture on the wall. So the picture got the most care.

After a few idle minutes the panel settles into standby: a large clock, the household's post-its, the shopping list, and the week ahead lying open on the map as seven columns with a fine line per day. And behind all of it, faintly, the streets of your own neighbourhood.

The map is not a texture. It is drawn once from OpenStreetMap around the place Home Assistant already knows, and the radius picks itself: a city centre gets its grid, a village its handful of lanes. Six places, the same wall.

Cologne. The Ring around the old town, the Rhine as a gap. Paris. Twelve avenues meeting at the Étoile. Barcelona. The Eixample grid, cut by the Diagonal. New York. Midtown Manhattan, Broadway crossing the grid. Amsterdam. The canal rings, drawn by their quays. Petting, Bavaria. A village: a handful of lanes, and that is the map.

  • Your streets, not a texture. The map is rendered once on the server, as one monochrome file that takes whatever colour the text has. Day and night share it, and when it rains outside, rain runs over it.
  • Everything on it is a door. Touch the shopping list and you are in the list. Touch the week and you are in the calendar. Touch a post-it and you are with that person's reminders. Nothing to unlock first.
  • It knows when to be quiet. Deep at night the screen keeps only a dimmed red clock. The hourly pixel shift that protects the panel is a soft slide, not a jump.

02 · The room

The illustration is the state.

Every room carries an illustration of itself, and the picture is the reading. When the sun sets, the evening picture fades in over the length of dusk. When a lamp is on, it is on in the picture. When the last one goes out, the room goes dark. When it rains outside, the window light turns grey and the rain runs down exactly where the glass is.

Beside the picture sits one narrow control surface: rooms, scenes, the lamps, the room's climate. It follows the sun too, so nobody in the household ever reaches for a theme setting. Try the four states.

Real captures of the same panel. Sun and rain by day, evening and lights off after dark; the controls change with it.

Or every room at once.

The same screen has a second view. Instead of one stage it shows all rooms as tiles, each with its own illustration, and the control surface steps out of the picture until you touch one. After dark the view earns its keep: a room with a lamp on carries its lit evening picture, a room with every lamp out carries the dark one. A green dot appears on a tile while a motion sensor in that room reports someone, and a room with a camera carries a quiet camera mark. Nobody reads a list to see which lights are still burning — the wall shows it from the hallway.

Six rooms by day. A tap puts one of them into the control surface. The same six after dark. Two rooms have a lamp on; the kitchen and the bathroom do not, and look it.

Photograph the room. That is the whole input.

The rooms that ship with Hauser are of one particular home, which is no use to yours. So the room-image wizard draws yours from a phone photograph: choose the crop and the focus point, and it returns the room as a set of pictures for sun, overcast, evening and lights off, with its windows already known, so the rain finds the glass.

Input Evening light, a wide lens, toys on the floor. Nothing staged.

Output The same room in the four states it ships in: sun, overcast, evening, every lamp out. This is the living room in the pictures above.

This is the one place where Hauser talks to a paid third party: the wizard needs your own OpenAI access, and your photo is sent there to be redrawn. That trade is stated before anything is uploaded, every paid step is confirmed by hand, and the wizard is not part of the public demo. It is an interim solution: for the iOS app a free way through Apple Intelligence is in development, and for the web app local models and your own keys for other providers are planned.

03 · The touch

Light physics.

Every command in Hauser has weight. Light comes on fast and goes out slowly, the way a room does. Nothing jumps; everything fades like dusk. The interface answers before the house does, and when the house disagrees, the control eases back to the truth instead of lying.

Reading lamp

On in a blink Off over the length of dusk

It answers before the house does

Tap a light and it is on, on screen, immediately. The command travels to Home Assistant afterwards. Every intent is tracked and reconciled against the state that comes back.

Undo instead of confirmation

"Everything off" does not ask whether you are sure. It goes out, and for five seconds a small strip offers the way back. Undo is a second command, not a dialog.

A pulse when the house is silent

If no answer arrives within a second, the control pulses softly until it does. If the house says no, the control fades back to the true state. Nothing snaps.

A moving blind stops with a tap

While a blind, shutter or garage door is on its way, a tap on its tile stops it where it is, like the switch on the wall. No second control, no confirmation.

The undo strip after a scene change. The gold bar is the five seconds running out.

Setting up

Configure on the blueprint. Then hand the panel over.

One mark in the middle of the title bar carries the mode: the h signet alone means use, the h with a gold dot means edit. In edit mode a long press on a room opens its configuration, and the room picture dissolves in place into a blueprint of itself — fine light lines on blue, computed from the picture you already have, so it works with your own photos too.

Devices are arranged by moving their tiles: hold one, drag it, the others glide aside. A small minus takes a device out of the room, a dashed tile opens the search, and adding a device celebrates with a gold check and six sparks. Rename the room where you set it up, pick its sensors, decide what its tile shows.

The living room's configuration on the panel: quick setup, the device order and the search, over the blueprint of the room.

Use mode closes every door.

Switch to use mode and the panel belongs to the household: lights, scenes, climate, blinds and everything else keep working, including a long press on a lamp for its brightness, but no configuration opens. The panel can fall back into use mode by itself after a while, and the way back to editing can sit behind a PIN. The mode belongs to the device: the hallway panel can be locked while your own phone keeps configuring.

Edit. The gold dot sits on the h; a long press opens configuration. Use. The h on its own; every control keeps working.

One hand, two voices

Paper for the household. Instruments for the house.

Everything a household says to itself is written on paper: post-its in a colour per person, the shopping list grouped by shop, the week as seven columns, the calendar as a sheet, the energy of the day as notes pinned into a picture. Everything you do to the house is an instrument: gold on a cool ground, tick scales, switches with travel. There is no third voice. No dashboard tiles, no generic grid.

And both voices write in one hand. Alegreya Sans carries everything you operate, Alegreya the paper — the greeting in standby, the notes, the calendar sheet, the energy figures. Figures stand upright and keep their width, so a clock or a slider value never jumps.

Reminders are post-its per person. The shopping list reads in the order you walk, and a ticked item stays under its shop for a day.

The calendar is the paper of the week band, only larger. Your Home Assistant calendars, one sheet.

The instruments.

Hold a lamp and its panel comes up: a tick scale for brightness, warm to cool, the colours it can take. Hold a fan and the panel shows what that fan can do and nothing it cannot: speed, presets, oscillation, direction. Hold the TV and, when Home Assistant places a remote beside it, a direction pad comes up. Every device gets the same hand, the same scale, the same weight under the finger.

The energy lives in the picture.

There is no column of numbers. Live load, generation and grid are notes pinned to the sun, the house and the pole in a picture of your home; the day's curve runs along the bottom edge in the same hand, and a small arc between sunrise and sunset says how far the day has walked. When it is cloudy, the house is drawn under an overcast sky, and rain falls over the picture. A second tap on Today lays the day's balance beside the wall: the share of your use that came from your own sun, the gap the grid filled, two bars on one kWh axis. What a house cannot measure is not shown: without a solar meter there is no solar note. No dash stands in for a number, anywhere.

Late afternoon under clouds. The notes hang on the panels, the wall and the pole; the sun arc says the day is nearly walked. A second tap on Today. How much of the day came from your own roof, and what the grid filled in.

Same language, different hand.

On the phone the rooms are the same cards the panel shows in its all-rooms view, and they go dark with the room in the evening. Navigation moves to the thumb as a floating bar. Under the rooms sits a quick bar with four fields that are yours: everything off, the central temperature, and buttons of your own that run scenes and devices from any room in one tap. A room opens as a sheet with its readings under the name — temperature, humidity, presence — and its climate behind the temperature. Configuring happens where you control: the picture dissolves into its blueprint and every tile stays where it is.

Home. The rooms, the quick bar, the bar under the thumb. A room. Its readings under the name, scenes and devices below. Configuring. The picture becomes its blueprint; the tiles stay where they are.

The iOS app

The same home in your pocket. With Apple Home or Home Assistant.

Hauser is also a native app for iPhone and iPad, drawn screen by screen in SwiftUI rather than wrapped around a web view: the same look, the same words, the same gestures. It runs three ways. With Home Assistant, paired to your Hauser installation by a QR code shown on the panel. Directly with Apple Home, with no server at all. Or with a built-in demo household, to look around before anything is connected. On an iPad it becomes the wall panel.

It is in TestFlight today, free, with a public link. Builds update in place, and what changes in each one follows the same release notes as the panel.

Widgets and a Live Activity

A room, a scene, a device, the home or the energy as a widget on the home screen. A lamp you switch on shows up as a Live Activity in the Dynamic Island until it is off again.

Siri, NFC and the share sheet

Add to the shopping list from anywhere with Siri or the share sheet. Stick an NFC tag by the door and a tap runs a scene. Leaving or arriving home can send a notification with buttons on it.

Your calendars, on the device

iCloud, Google and Exchange calendars of the phone merge with the events the server knows. The shopping list sorts itself into shop sections on the device with Apple Intelligence, no subscription.

Offline and private

Ticks on the shopping list and reminders queue while you are out of reach and land when you are back. Documents open behind Face ID. Nothing leaves your home but the pairing with your own server.

The app is a project of its own beside the panel. It needs iOS 17 or newer; the glass surfaces come with iOS 26, and the features that use Apple Intelligence need a device that supports it.

Weakness with dignity

A wall clock never goes out.

When Home Assistant cannot be reached, the stage stays lit. The controls dim, the connection state is one quiet word in the title bar, and nothing stacks a banner over the room. In standby the clock, the date and the week keep going from the panel itself.

Empty states are designed like the rest: a clock instead of a message, leaving out instead of a dash, a mood instead of a warning. A disconnected home should look like a quiet one, not a broken one.

Technical beta

Built in the open, for one household first.

Hauser installs and runs today. Compatibility with the enormous range of Home Assistant setups is still growing, there is no support SLA, and it is built for trusted deployments on your own network rather than for the public internet. Which version is current, and what changed in it, is in the release notes; the iOS app follows the same beta in TestFlight.

What you get

  • A complete, working interface you can run and read
  • A documented design system, interaction contract and performance budget
  • A guided Home Assistant setup with versioned external configuration
  • A stress house before every release: the built app is set up against a simulated Home Assistant full of real-world pitfalls and clicked through end to end, in the web app and in the iOS app
  • AGPL licensed: fork it, run it, modify it; share your changes if you offer it to others

Worth knowing

  • No SLA and no promised response time
  • Device and Home Assistant coverage grows release by release
  • Intended for trusted local networks, not for internet exposure
  • Versions move fast; read the release notes before upgrading
  • The iOS app is a TestFlight beta; its builds expire after ninety days and are replaced by the next one

Installations outside this household exist and report back. The first one ran on an Asustor NAS with Docker Compose against Home Assistant Container, with the first light under control ten minutes in. Reports like that, on GitHub and in the Home Assistant community, shape the next release; the full picture lives in the release notes and the README.

Install

One click on Home Assistant OS. Compose everywhere else. TestFlight on the phone.

Both server paths end in the same guided setup: it scans Home Assistant, proposes rooms and devices, lets you correct the result and activates a validated configuration in one step. No source edits, no YAML. The iOS app pairs to that installation — or skips it and talks to Apple Home.
